Using Domains as LLM Consumers

In that work, I notice a pattern. LLM-assisted work starts off going well — and then, gradually, progressively, goes sideways in ways that are hard to name in the moment. You just notice that the suggestions are getting off, or you're spending more time correcting than building, or you keep typing some version of "no, that's not what I meant." I want to show you two examples right at the top so you know exactly what I'm talking about. On the left: you ask about "the model." You know what you mean. The LLM picks a meaning too, confidently. It's the wrong one. Three messages before you're back on track. On the right: you set a constraint at message 3. Clear, explicit, well-stated. Thirty-seven messages later, the LLM is violating it. Not because it forgot. Because the constraint got buried under everything else. I think we can apply a framework from domain-driven design — specifically, bounded contexts — to these problems, and look to it for concrete practices for fixing them.
I. Setup: What Bounded Contexts Give Us

A bounded context is a semantic boundary

Adjacent systems can use the same word to mean different things. That's fine. Assuming they agree without checking is not.

A bounded context is a concept from domain-driven design — specifically from Eric Evans's work. The core idea is that you can draw a boundary around a region of your system, and inside that boundary, terms have precise, agreed-upon meanings. "Customer" means one specific thing here. "Order" means one specific thing here. You've established a ubiquitous language — a shared vocabulary that everyone working inside that context uses consistently. Here's the crucial flip side: you explicitly do NOT assume that vocabulary transfers outside the boundary. Adjacent systems, adjacent teams, can use the same words to mean completely different things. That's not a problem — it's expected. The problem only arises when you forget the boundaries exist and act like "Customer" in Billing is the same object as "Customer" in Sales. I want to spend two more slides on this because the concrete example makes the whole rest of the talk much clearer.
I. Setup: What Bounded Contexts Give Us

Same word. Three different models.

Here's the classic example. The word "Customer" appears everywhere in most software systems. But it doesn't mean the same thing in every part of those systems. In Sales, a customer is a lead — someone with a pipeline stage, a lead score, a potential value. That object doesn't have a shipping address. It doesn't have a payment method. It has a probability of converting and a contact history. In Shipping, the same word means something completely different: an address, an order record, a set of delivery preferences. Nobody in Shipping cares about lead scores. They care about whether the address is valid and what the return history looks like. In Billing, it's a payment account — a tax ID, credit status, invoice history. Domain-driven design's answer to this is not: pick one definition and force everyone to use it. That causes the same kind of pain as trying to write code that serves every use case with a single class. DDD says: let these diverge. They're three different models that happen to share a name in English. Own that. Name the contexts. Establish the vocabulary within each one deliberately. And — critically — don't assume vocabulary from one context means the same thing in another. That's the payoff I want to talk about next.
I. Setup: What Bounded Contexts Give Us

The payoff: scoped, coherent conversation

The problem isn't ambiguous words. The problem is unacknowledged ambiguous words — everyone assumes the other person means the same thing they do.
The payoff of bounded contexts is really about scoped, coherent conversation. When you've established the ubiquitous language within a context — when you've said "in this team, 'Customer' means X" — you get precise conversations. Your questions are precise. The answers you get back are precise. And when you're working with someone from a different bounded context, you know to make the translation explicit. You don't assume that their "Customer" is your "Customer." The key insight here — and I want to sit on this for a second because the rest of the talk depends on it — is that the problem isn't ambiguous words per se. Ambiguity is unavoidable. The problem is unacknowledged ambiguity. It's when both parties are confident they mean the same thing and they don't. That's where you get the really expensive bugs: the kind where everything looks correct on both sides of the integration and the data is silently wrong. Keep that framing in mind: unacknowledged ambiguity. Because that's exactly what happens when you start working with LLMs without bounded context discipline.
II. The Curse: Why LLMs Encourage the Opposite

The interface invites unboundedness

▊

no boundary set
no scope defined
no vocabulary established
just: keep typing

msgs 1–5 · debug a function
msg 6 · is this architecture even right?
msgs 7–11 · redesign the ingestion layer
msgs 12–18 · write tests for the original fn
msg 19 · wait, what about that other file?
still one conversation.
Here's the problem. LLMs, as they're currently presented to us, feel like one big infinite conversation. The interface is a text box and a blinking cursor. There's no tooling that teaches you context discipline. There's no prompt that says "wait, are you in the same domain as 10 messages ago?" There's nothing that surfaces when a conversation has grown so broad that it's lost coherence. The blinking cursor is a UI affordance. It's there because the designers wanted you to feel invited to type. But it has this side effect of implying: this is a continuous, coherent context, and you should just keep adding to it. It doesn't say: scope this first. It doesn't say: establish your vocabulary. It says: keep going. And so we get this pattern that I think most of us recognize. You open a conversation to debug a function. That surfaces an architecture question, which you ask. That leads you to think about test coverage, so you ask about that too. Then you remember a completely different file you've been meaning to look at, and you ask about that. Still one conversation. And you've just built a context that spans four different problem domains without establishing the vocabulary for any of them. That's the curse. The interface creates unboundedness, and then we pay for it. Let me show you the four specific ways it costs us.
III. Failure Mode 1: No Ubiquitous Language

Language mismatch at the start

  • You ask about "the model," confident it's clear
  • The LLM picks its own meaning, also confident
  • Neither of you flags the ambiguity
  • Three suggestions deep before you notice the mismatch
The first failure mode is language mismatch at the start. This is the most common one, and it's the most insidious because it's invisible. You ask about "the model": perfectly natural thing to say in an engineering conversation. You know exactly which model you mean. The LLM picks a meaning. Also confidently. Maybe it picks your ML pipeline because that's what it figures engineers mean in this kind of conversation. Maybe it picks the ORM layer because you mentioned a database two messages ago. Maybe it picks a view model because you showed it some React code. Neither party flags the ambiguity. You're both confident. The LLM gives you a perfectly coherent response about the model as it understood it, and you read it through the lens of the model you meant. And it sounds... almost right? But the suggestions don't quite fit. They're solving a slightly different problem than you thought you asked. And you don't figure out why until you're three or four suggestions deep and have already integrated some of them into your thinking. This is exactly what happens in inter-team software work too. Two teams use "Customer" all the time. They both know what it means. They both know the other team uses it. And nobody thinks to check whether they mean the same thing until there's a production incident. Here's the fix.
III. Failure Mode 1: Fix

The Terminology Block

Write your ubiquitous language before you ask your first question.

// Before asking anything:
"In this conversation:
  'model' = our scikit-learn inference pipeline
  'feature' = a column in the feature store
  'deployment' = a push to the staging cluster"
How to detect the mismatch: At the first surprise, ask "what did you understand 'X' to mean?"
30 seconds at the start. Takes less time than the first correction will.
The fix is what I call a terminology block. Before you ask your first substantive question, write 3-5 lines defining the vocabulary that could be ambiguous in your context. You are literally writing the ubiquitous language of your bounded conversation. "In this conversation, 'model' means our scikit-learn inference pipeline. 'Feature' means a column in the feature store. 'Deployment' means a push to staging." Done. That's your context definition. It takes 30 seconds. It takes less time than the first correction will. And it frames the entire conversation before any ambiguity has had a chance to propagate. There's also a reactive version of this tip. If you didn't do the terminology block and you notice the first response doesn't quite fit — don't correct it immediately. Instead, ask: "What did you understand 'model' to mean in that answer?" Let it show you its vocabulary. You'll see the gap clearly in the answer. Then you correct it once, explicitly, and move forward with the vocabulary aligned. The pattern I want to avoid is the one where you correct the surface-level answer — "no, not like that" — without fixing the underlying vocabulary mismatch. That just makes the LLM try harder to give you the same kind of answer. Fix the vocabulary first.
III. Failure Mode 2: Context Degrades Its Own Signal

Detail loss mid-conversation

  • msg 3: "never use exceptions for flow control"
  • msg 40: LLM suggests try/except for flow control
  • The constraint didn't vanish. It got buried.
  • The more context you add, the less signal each early message carries
The second failure mode is detail loss mid-conversation. This one feels different from language mismatch — it's not about ambiguous vocabulary, it's about constraints you've clearly stated being progressively overridden by the weight of everything else you've added since. Here's the picture I want you to hold. You state a constraint at message 3. "Never use exceptions for flow control in this codebase." Clear, unambiguous. But as the conversation grows — architecture discussions, test examples, configuration questions — that constraint's relative weight in the context decreases. It's not gone. It's just getting buried under everything else, and by message 40, the LLM is suggesting exactly what you said not to do. Not because it forgot. Because the constraint's signal is now very low relative to the mass of everything else. The chart I'm showing here isn't literal — we don't actually have a metric for constraint signal strength. But the shape is real. The longer the conversation, the more the early specific constraints get diluted by the general mass of later content. This is actually a property of how attention works in these models. Every message competes for influence on the output. An early constraint competes with 37 later messages. It often loses.
III. Failure Mode 2: Context Degrades Its Own Signal

A session without context discipline

The constraint didn't disappear from the window. It got buried under everything else.
Let me walk through a concrete version of what this looks like. You open a conversation to debug a data pipeline function. Fine. By message 6 you've pivoted to an architecture question about scale. That's a different domain. You've mentioned along the way — at message 3, let's say — that in this codebase you never use exceptions for flow control. Important constraint, stated clearly. By message 12 you're redesigning the whole ingestion layer. Different domain again. You've asked it to write tests. You've asked it about a deployment configuration. And by message 40, when you get back to the implementation, the LLM is cheerfully suggesting try/except as a flow control mechanism. Exactly the thing you said never to do. The constraint didn't disappear. It's still in the context window. It just got buried under thirty-seven messages worth of architecture discussion and test writing and deployment config, and its signal weight relative to everything else is now very low. This is the curse in action. One conversation, many domains, no ubiquitous language established in any of them, constraints eroding under the weight of everything else. Let me break this down into four specific failure modes with names.
III. Failure Mode 2: Fix

Constraints first. Re-anchor later.

Message 1
all constraints
that must hold
for the whole
conversation
re-anchor @ msg 15–20
re-anchor @ msg 35–40

Constraints buried mid-conversation are effectively invisible by message 30.

The Re-Anchor: Every 15–20 messages in a long conversation, send one message restating your key constraints and current goal explicitly.

"Reminding you: no exceptions for flow control, targeting Python 3.9, goal is still X."
Signal: You've written some version of "remember, we said X" more than once. That's a signal strength problem.
Two fixes here. The first is simple: any constraint that needs to hold for the entire conversation should be the first message, or part of the first message, not message 3. Constraints introduced mid-conversation start at a signal disadvantage. Put them first, before anything else has had a chance to dilute them. The second fix is the re-anchor. In conversations that run past 15-20 messages, periodically send one message that restates the key constraints and the current goal. "Reminding you: we're not using exceptions for flow control. Python 3.9 target. Goal is still X." This is not redundant. This is maintenance. You're deliberately refreshing the signal weight of the constraints that matter. Here's the detection heuristic for when you need to re-anchor: you've written some version of "remember, we said..." more than once. That's not a comprehension failure on the LLM's part. It's a signal strength failure. The fix isn't to correct more clearly — the fix is to re-anchor. There's a difference between "the model doesn't understand my constraint" and "my constraint has been diluted by context mass." The second one is fixed by re-anchoring, not by phrasing the correction more forcefully. And I want to call out a framing shift here. The re-anchor is also useful for you. Every 15-20 messages is a good time to ask yourself whether the constraints you set at message 3 still reflect what you actually want. Context evolves. You're allowed to update your constraints. The re-anchor is a forcing function for that.
III. Failure Mode 3: One Context, Multiple Domains

Goals bleeding into each other

  • Exploration and implementation in the same thread
  • Brainstorming and deciding in the same thread
  • The model anchors on early brainstorm ideas when you're trying to make a final call
  • Suggestions come out half-explanation, half-implementation, uncommitted to either
The third failure mode is goals bleeding into each other. You start exploring an unfamiliar codebase — "what does this function do, what's the intent here, what would break if we changed it?" Great. Exploration mode. But then you see something that seems worth fixing right now, and you ask the LLM to rewrite it. And now you're in implementation mode. In the same conversation. With the same framing. The result is responses that are half-explanation and half-suggestion, uncommitted to either register. The LLM doesn't know whether you want to understand the system or change it, so it tries to do both and does neither very well. The explanations come with caveats about implementation. The suggestions come with qualifications about "you might want to consider." It hedges because the framing is mixed. There's a related version of this with brainstorming and deciding. You ask it to brainstorm approaches to a problem. Good — you get a list of five options with tradeoffs. But then in the same conversation you say "ok, which one should we use?" And the LLM anchors on the ideas it already generated. It's not evaluating fresh — it's picking from the brainstorm with the same framing it used to generate it. You get a choice that's influenced by the brainstorm's framing, not an independent evaluation. Two different modes of engagement. They should be two different conversations.
III. Failure Mode 3: Fix

Declare your mode before you ask anything

Exploring
"I want to understand this before touching anything."
Brainstorming
"Generate options: I'll evaluate them separately."
Deciding
"I need a recommendation with a rationale, not a list."
Implementing
"The decision is made. Help me build it."
When you shift modes, start a new conversation. Carry forward only conclusions, not the path that produced them.
The fix is to declare your mode before you ask your first question. Four modes worth naming explicitly. Exploring: you want to understand before you touch anything. The LLM should describe, explain, surface tradeoffs. Not suggest changes. If you tell it "I'm in exploration mode, I don't want suggestions yet," it can calibrate to that. Brainstorming: you want options without evaluation. Tell it that. You're generating a possibility space, not choosing. If you don't tell it, it'll pre-evaluate and narrow for you, which defeats the purpose. Deciding: you want a recommendation with a rationale. Not a list of five options. Not "it depends." A position with reasoning. Ask for that explicitly. Implementing: the decision is made. You're executing, not reconsidering. Tell it that so it doesn't keep offering alternatives. The bigger principle here: when you shift from one mode to another, start a new conversation. And carry forward only the conclusions — not the path. If your exploration conversation established that the bottleneck is in the query layer, start a new conversation with "we've determined the bottleneck is in the query layer. Now help me implement an index on user_id." The exploration conversation is done. This is an implementation conversation. Keep them separate.
III. Failure Mode 4: Trying to Refactor a Bloated Context

Can't change course

  • 20 messages designing a REST API
  • "Actually, let's switch to GraphQL"
  • REST patterns keep creeping back, not because it forgot, but because the context is saturated with REST
  • You keep writing "no, not like that" and it keeps not quite sticking
The fourth failure mode is the one that's most frustrating because it feels like you should be able to fix it with the right phrasing. You've been designing a REST API for 20 messages — resource modeling, HTTP verbs, status codes, pagination patterns. Good conversation. Then you realize you actually need to switch to GraphQL. So you say so. And REST patterns keep creeping back. The LLM acknowledges the switch — sure, GraphQL — and then gives you a response shaped by REST thinking. You say "no, not like that, I mean the GraphQL way." It tries again. Closer but still influenced by the REST framing. You're writing "no, not like that" for the third time, and it's still not quite landing. Here's what's happening: the context is saturated with REST. 80% of the conversation is REST content. Your GraphQL pivot is a small signal competing against a massive REST prior. You can't reason the model out of this through correction, because you're not dealing with a comprehension problem. You're dealing with an architectural problem. The context itself is wrong. This is exactly what DDD describes when it talks about trying to translate between bounded contexts without an anti-corruption layer. The old model bleeds through because it's everywhere.
III. Failure Mode 4: Fix

Pivots are boundary events, not corrections

"Let's switch to GraphQL"
after 20 messages of REST design

is not
asking it to change its mind
is
asking it to replace its context

You cannot reason a model out of a saturated context.

What to do: Start a new conversation. Lead with the destination. Don't mention the journey.

"We're building a GraphQL API. Schema-first design, the data layer is PostgreSQL, resolvers in Python."

The REST conversation is over. This is a GraphQL conversation.
Signal: You've written "no, not like that" more than twice in a row. Stop correcting. Start fresh.
The fix is to recognize pivots as context-boundary events, not corrections. "Let's switch to GraphQL" after 20 messages of REST is not asking the LLM to change its mind about something. It's asking it to replace its context. Those are fundamentally different operations. Corrections can work within a context. Context replacement requires a new context. So the answer is: start a new conversation. And crucially, lead with the destination, not the journey. Don't open the new conversation with "we were designing a REST API but now we're switching to GraphQL." That just smuggles the REST context into the new conversation. Open it with: "We're building a GraphQL API. Schema-first design. Data layer is PostgreSQL. Resolvers in Python." That's your new context. That's where you start. The REST conversation isn't wasted — you may have learned things from it. But carry forward only what's still true. The useful output of the REST conversation might be "we need pagination, we need auth at the resource level, we need versioning." Those are requirements that travel. The REST-specific modeling decisions don't travel. Leave them behind. Detection: you've written "no, not like that" more than twice in a row. At that point, stop correcting. You are not going to fix this through better phrasing. Start fresh.

I've been naming fixes as I went through the failure modes, but I want to collect them here as a set of six practices you can use deliberately. I've already covered the Mode Declaration and the Re-Anchor. Let me walk through the other four with a bit more depth. The reason I want to name these as practices rather than just tips is that I think framing matters here. These aren't workarounds for a bad tool. They're the same discipline that makes you a good collaborator in any bounded context — with a human team, with an external API, with a documentation system. The LLM is a collaborator with its own domain model, and you need to manage the boundary between your model and its model. That framing, I think, is more durable than a list of tips. The tips will evolve as the tooling evolves. The boundary discipline is always relevant.
IV. Practices

The Conversation Charter

2–3 sentences at the start of any non-trivial conversation:

// 1. Scope — what system / module / problem
"We are working on the authentication module of a Python Flask app."

// 2. Vocabulary — ambiguous terms in this context
"'token' means a JWT, not a payment token."

// 3. Mode — exploring, brainstorming, deciding, or implementing?
"We are designing — not implementing yet."
This is exactly what a bounded context definition looks like. You are doing DDD, not just prompting.
The Conversation Charter is the most high-leverage practice in this list. It's three things, written at the start of any non-trivial conversation. First: scope. What system, module, or problem are we working in? "We are working on the authentication module of a Python Flask app." This orients everything. It's the bounded context name. Second: vocabulary. Which terms in this conversation could mean multiple things? "In this conversation, 'token' means a JWT — not a payment token." You don't need to define every term. Just the ones that could go wrong. Third: mode. Are we exploring, brainstorming, deciding, or implementing? "We are designing, not implementing yet." This tells the LLM what register to respond in. Three sentences. Maybe four. Thirty seconds. And then you ask your question. I want to be explicit about what this practice actually is. It's not a prompting trick. It's a bounded context definition. You are saying: here is the boundary. Here is the vocabulary within it. Here is the purpose of this collaboration. You are doing domain-driven design with your conversation. The fact that it happens to make LLM interactions much more productive is a consequence, not the goal.
IV. Practices

One conversation, one domain

Starting a new conversation is not losing context. It's maintaining integrity. You choose what crosses the boundary.
The useful question at any pivot: "Am I in a new domain now?"
If yes — this is a new conversation.
The second practice follows directly from the charter. Split conversations the way you'd split bounded contexts. Research and implementation are different domains — separate conversations. Exploration and rewriting are different domains — separate conversations. Brainstorming and deciding are different domains — separate conversations. I know this feels like it will slow you down. It won't. What will slow you down is spending 20 minutes arguing with a saturated context. A fresh conversation with a clean charter is faster than five corrections in a confused one. The key reframe here: starting a new conversation is not losing context. It's maintaining integrity. You are choosing what crosses the boundary deliberately, just like the DDD anti-corruption layer — rather than letting everything bleed across implicitly. The useful question to ask yourself at any point where you feel yourself pivoting: "Am I in a new domain now?" If the answer is yes — if you've shifted from understanding to building, from generating options to picking one, from the old architecture to the new one — that's a new conversation. Stop and start fresh. It takes ten seconds to open a new window. The charter takes 30 more. You're at net positive within two messages.
IV. Practices

The Carry-Only-Conclusions Handoff

When you start a new conversation after closing one:

"Previous conversation established:
  — the bottleneck is in the query layer
  — we're adding an index on user_id
  — we're not changing the ORM
Build from here."
When you close one conversation and open another, the question is: what do you carry forward? The Carry-Only-Conclusions Handoff answers that. Write 2-4 bullet points: the decisions that were made, the constraints that still apply, the direction that's been set. "Previous conversation established: the bottleneck is in the query layer, we're adding an index on user_id, we're not touching the ORM. Build from here." That's the handoff. What you deliberately exclude: the reasoning, the alternatives you considered, the wrong turns, the exploration. Those belong to the old conversation. They don't cross the boundary. This is exactly what DDD calls context-mapping — the explicit, deliberate translation of concepts from one bounded context to another. In DDD, the context map is a formal artifact that the teams maintain. Here, it's a three-line block at the top of a new conversation. The principle is identical. And this has a nice side effect: it forces you to be clear about what was actually decided. If you can't write the conclusions in 3-4 bullets, you may not have actually finished the last conversation. That's valuable information — sometimes the blocker isn't the implementation, it's that you haven't actually made the decision yet.

The Anti-Corruption Layer Moment

✗

⛔

This is not a clarification problem. It's a boundary problem. In DDD, some vocabulary cannot be translated — it can only be replaced.

Third time rule: If you've corrected the same thing three times and it hasn't stuck, stop correcting. The anti-corruption layer isn't working because you haven't drawn a new boundary yet. Start a new conversation.
Trying to fix a boundary problem through better clarification is like trying to fix a schema conflict by writing clearer error messages.
I've already named this one in the failure modes, but it deserves its own practice slot because it's the one people most often try to solve by trying harder. Here's the situation: you've corrected the same misunderstanding three times. Different phrasing each time. Still not landing. The natural impulse is to try one more correction, more carefully worded. Don't. In domain-driven design, the anti-corruption layer is the mechanism that prevents one bounded context's model from leaking into another. It's a translation layer at the boundary. And the reason it exists is that some vocabulary genuinely cannot be translated — it can only be replaced. There's no good way to map the Sales "Customer" onto the Shipping "Customer" because they're fundamentally different objects. The anti-corruption layer prevents you from even trying. When you're writing the same correction for the third time, you're experiencing this. The correction isn't sticking because you're asking the model to translate between two incompatible framings within the same saturated context. There's no translation path. The only fix is a new boundary. The third time rule: if you've corrected the same thing three times and it hasn't landed, stop. Start a new conversation. Lead with where you want to be, not with where you came from.
V. The Useful Flip Side

Where this framing tells us LLMs will shine

Tasks with naturally tight, well-defined domains:

"Convert this Python fn to TypeScript"
bounded input · bounded output · no vocabulary to negotiate
"Write a regex that matches this format"
clear success condition · verifiable output
"Generate a docstring for this function"
the function is the domain · self-contained
"Reformat this SQL to match the style guide"
style guide = ubiquitous language · no ambiguity
These are good LLM tasks even for one-off uses where you'd never bother building a specialized tool. The domain is already bounded.
Here's the useful flip side of this whole framework. If bounded context discipline is what makes LLM work coherent, then this framing tells us exactly where LLMs are most useful: tasks that are naturally bounded. Tasks where the domain is tight and well-defined, the vocabulary is unambiguous, and the success condition is clear. Convert this Python function to TypeScript. The domain is the function. The vocabulary is Python and TypeScript. The success condition is: a TypeScript function that does the same thing. There's nothing to negotiate. You don't need a charter for this. Just ask. Write a regex that matches this format. The domain is the format. The success condition is verifiable — does the regex match the examples? No ambiguity in "customer" here. These are not just good LLM tasks. They're tasks where you get almost all of the benefit with almost none of the context discipline overhead. The domain is already bounded before you open the conversation. And I want to be explicit: this is why one-shot LLM uses feel so satisfying compared to long, evolving conversations. It's not that the model is better at short tasks. It's that short tasks are more likely to be naturally bounded. The frustration comes from trying to do long, multi-domain work with the same interface.
V. The Useful Flip Side

Where they'll always struggle

  • Tasks where the domain is still being discovered
  • "Help me design a system that does X, Y, and Z": domain is negotiated as you go
  • These aren't unsolvable; they require more context discipline, not less
  • The friction is architectural, not conversational. Better phrasing won't fix it.
Here's the 2x2 that I think captures the relationship cleanly. Two axes: domain clarity on the horizontal — how tight and well-defined is the problem domain — and success condition clarity on the vertical — how clearly can you evaluate the output? Top right: tight domain, clear success condition. This is the LLM sweet spot. Regex, type conversion, docstring generation, test scaffolding. Go here without hesitation. Top left: fuzzy domain, clear success condition. This is risky. You have a clear idea of what the output should look like, but the domain hasn't been established. The model will generate something that looks right and isn't. It fills the domain vacuum with confidence. This is where hallucination lives. Bottom right: tight domain, fuzzy success condition. Good with structure. Refactoring to a pattern, style conformance, code review. You need iteration but the domain is well-bounded — the context discipline work is lighter. Bottom left: fuzzy domain, fuzzy success condition. Architecture design, system exploration, multi-domain work. These tasks require the most context discipline, and they're also the tasks where LLMs are most often used carelessly. Not impossible — just expensive in context management overhead. The domain is being discovered as you go. The corollary for tool selection: if a task is in the top right, LLMs are great — even for one-shot uses. If it's in the bottom left, the LLM isn't the bottleneck. Your ability to define the context is.
